## Title
Detecting hate in diversity: a survey of multilingual code-mixed image and video analysis

## Authors
Hafiz Muhammad Raza Ur Rehman; Mahpara Saleem; Muhammad Zeeshan Jhandir; Eduardo Silva Alvarado; Helena Garay; Imran Ashraf (Raza Ur Rehman and Saleem contributed equally)

## DOI

## Research Objective
"The primary aim of this work is to conduct a comprehensive analysis of hate speech identification in textual, audio, and visual formats ... taking into account the difficulties associated with code-mixing and multilingual environments." The survey covers hate speech detection across code-mixed, multilingual, visual, audio, and textual scenarios, classifies types of hate speech, compares methods (NLP, ML, DL), and proposes a framework combining cross-lingual models and multimodal data fusion.

## Language(s)
Multilingual coverage. Reviewed datasets span English, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Indonesian, Arabic, Dutch, German, French, Greek, Amharic, Marathi, and Hindi-English / Urdu-English code-mixed (Roman script). English is noted as the most commonly studied language.

## Task
Survey of hate speech detection (including its subtypes: abusive language, cyberbullying, sexism, racism, radicalization, extremism, toxic language) in text, audio, video, image, multilingual, and code-mixed settings on social media. Underlying detection tasks in reviewed works are mostly binary/multi-class classification.

## Model(s)
Surveyed method families: NLP techniques (dictionary/lexicon-based, BoW, N-grams, TF-IDF, sentiment analysis, part-of-speech, rule-based, template-based), Machine Learning (SVM, Random Forest, Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, KNN, Decision Tree, GBDT), and Deep Learning (CNN, LSTM, GRU, RNN, DNN). Transformer/embedding models referenced: BERT, multilingual BERT (mBERT), XLM-R, RoBERTa, FastText, word2vec, GloVe, doc2vec, and the Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP) vision-language model. Practical algorithms detailed: RoBERTa [119], Cross-platform hate speech detection [120], and the HARE framework [121].

## Architecture Type
Survey / review paper. It proposes (conceptually, without experiments) a novel framework integrating cross-lingual representations (multilingual BERT or XLM-R) with vision-language multimodal fusion (CLIP) for combined text + image/video analysis to handle code-switching, linguistic variety, and visual ambiguity.

## Preprocessing Pipeline
Described generically: data collection via web scraping/APIs (respecting terms of service) from Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, Instagram; manual or automatic annotation of hate presence with ethical/privacy considerations; text preprocessing including removing stop words, stemming, and lemmatization. For audio/video: extract audio from video, convert audio to text (ASR), then classify with text-based features. Audio feature extraction via Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) and Chroma Vectors; noise reduction and ASR customization for accented/colloquial speech. Code-mixed feature examples: punctuation/emoticon counts, character and word N-grams, word2vec of lexicon words, chi-square dimensionality reduction, LIWC and sentiment score features.

## Main Results
Key survey findings:
- SVM remains one of the most widely used models; TF-IDF features are prevalent in prior research.
- English is the most commonly studied language; Twitter is the most frequently used data source (Table 9).
- Deep learning models consistently outperform traditional ML models (SVM, Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, Random Forest), especially when using word2vec, GloVe, and FastText; ensemble deep learning models often yield even better results.
Representative reported numbers from reviewed studies:
- [90] Hindi-English code-mixed: SVM 71.7% accuracy, RF 66.7% accuracy.
- [45] sub-word level LSTM 69.8% accuracy; attention-based hierarchical LSTM on phonemic sub-words 66.6% accuracy.
- Table 9 examples (accuracy, precision, recall, F1): [122] YouTube rule-based 0.98/0.94; [108] Naive Bayes 0.97/0.82; [51] Twitter SVM/CNN etc. 0.93/0.93/0.93; [2] SVM 0.88/0.87/0.87; [34] LSTM+SVM Facebook 0.833/0.872/0.851; [126] Amharic LSTM/GRU recall 0.97, F1 0.92; [125] Dutch SVM 0.49/0.43/0.46.

## Strengths
- PRISMA-based methodology with explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria and screening counts.
- Broad, integrative scope spanning text, audio, video, images, multilingual, and code-mixed data — explicitly broader than prior single-modality/English-only surveys (comparative Table 4).
- Provides consolidated definitional tables (hate speech vs. related concepts; platform definitions), dataset tables, and method-vs-metric comparison tables.
- Includes practical real-world application domains (news, e-commerce, education, social media, online gaming) and challenges/solutions/future-directions synthesis.

## Weaknesses
- Focused on hate speech (not fake news); relevance to a fake-news benchmark is indirect.
- The proposed framework is conceptual and not experimentally validated (no results, ablations, or datasets for it).
- Some reported screening numbers appear internally inconsistent (e.g., 1500 identified, "800 excluded" as duplicates yet "900 unique papers").
- Aggregated performance numbers come from heterogeneous, non-comparable settings; several table cells are incomplete.
- Data availability is restricted ("dataset can be requested from the authors").

## Limitations
Stated challenges/limitations (Challenges section): no universally accepted definition of hate speech across cultures; difficulty with implicit hate speech and sarcasm requiring deep contextual/cultural understanding; code-switching complicates detection with traditional models; dataset credibility issues (many self-generated datasets, single-user dominance, lack of domain generalization); under-representation of minority languages/dialects causing biased outcomes; predominance of supervised learning; and the "black box" opacity of ML/DL models limiting transparency.

## Future Work
Stated future directions:
- Multi-modal analysis integrating text, audio, and visual data (voice tone, gestures, facial expressions).
- Creating comprehensive, balanced, standardized multilingual and code-mixed hate-speech datasets; refined annotation methods accounting for mixed languages and cultural differences.
- Harnessing unlabeled data via unsupervised/semi-supervised learning; strategies for imbalanced datasets (advanced sampling, cost-sensitive learning, ensembles).
- Further exploration of deep learning models and continuous model updating for evolving hate-speech patterns.
- Enhancing interpretability via attention-based mechanisms and explainable AI (XAI); integrating non-textual elements (images, emojis).

## Interesting Ideas
- Vision-language multimodal fusion (CLIP) combined with cross-lingual encoders (mBERT/XLM-R) for jointly handling code-mixing and visual ambiguity.
- Illustration of how code-mixing can amplify or alter sentiment/hate intensity (Table 5 examples with a 0–2 hate-intensity scale).
- Audio-visual pipeline: extract audio → ASR transcription → text-feature classification, augmented with MFCC/Chroma and emotion recognition (anger/disgust cues).
- Cross-platform detection (YouTube/Twitter/Gab) and the HARE explainable framework.

## Possible Inspiration For Our Paper
- Reinforces the importance of multilingual and code-mixed evaluation conditions — our fake-news benchmark could include non-English and code-mixed splits.
- Motivates a multimodal (text + image/video + audio) dimension and an explainability (XAI) component in benchmark design.
- The method-vs-feature-vs-metric comparison tables (Tables 8–9) are a useful template for organizing our benchmark's model/feature/metric matrix.
- Standard metric set (accuracy, precision, recall, F1, AUC, Cohen's Kappa) transfers directly to fake-news classification reporting.

## Potential Citation Usage
- Cite as a 2025 survey establishing methodological conventions (SVM/TF-IDF historically dominant; DL and transformer/embedding methods now outperforming; ensembles best) in adjacent misinformation/harmful-content detection.
- Cite for the argument that datasets are English-centric and that multilingual/code-mixed resources are scarce — supporting a call for more diverse benchmarks.
- Cite for multimodal and explainability motivations, and for the CLIP + mBERT/XLM-R fusion idea.
- Use as a related-work bridge showing overlap between hate-speech and fake-news detection pipelines (shared features, models, and evaluation practices).

## Reviewer Notes
Adjacent-domain survey (hate speech, not fake news) but methodologically transferable to the benchmark. Best used for framing multilingual/code-mixed/multimodal motivations and metric standardization rather than as a source of fake-news-specific results. Treat aggregated accuracies (e.g., SVM 71.7%, LSTM 69.8%) as secondary, originating from heterogeneous primary studies. Note the proposed CLIP + XLM-R framework is untested, and screening statistics contain a minor inconsistency; verify before quoting exact counts.
